## About

New England Aster is one of the showiest and most ecologically important wildflowers of the eastern United States. Despite its name, it is native throughout much of central and eastern North America, found in moist meadows, roadsides, and open areas. Plants grow 3–6 feet tall and produce masses of vivid purple daisy-like flowers with golden-yellow centers in late summer and fall — one of the most spectacular autumn wildflower displays available to Northeast gardeners. The flowers are sticky and the stems are hairy. It is a long-lived, robust perennial that spreads by rhizome to form clumps.

## Wildlife Value

New England Aster is a cornerstone fall wildlife plant. It is one of the most critical nectar sources for monarch butterflies migrating south in September and October, and also a major late-season resource for bumblebee queens fattening up before winter. It is a host plant for the Pearl Crescent butterfly. The seeds are eaten by songbirds, and the dense clumps provide winter cover for insects and small animals.

## In the Treewell

New England Aster can get quite tall — 4–6 feet in rich soil — and may benefit from pinching back in early summer (by about one-third) to keep it more compact and bushy. It will spread by rhizome over time, potentially becoming a dominant presence in the treewell. The spectacular fall display of purple flowers, timed to coincide with monarch migration, makes it one of the highest-value plants in the entire mix.

## Growing Notes

- **Type:** Perennial
- **Height:** 3–6 ft
- **Bloom time:** August–October
- **Sun needs:** Full sun to part shade
- **Water needs:** Moderate; prefers average to moist soils

## Seedling Identification

Cotyledons are small and oval. The first true leaves are lance-shaped to spatula-shaped, alternate, and hairy — with a rough texture similar to Black-Eyed Susan. The distinctive feature that sets New England Aster apart from other hairy-leaved seedlings is the clasping leaf base: each leaf wraps around the stem slightly at its base rather than tapering cleanly to a petiole. This clasping base is visible even at 2–3 leaf pairs if you look at where the leaf meets the stem.
