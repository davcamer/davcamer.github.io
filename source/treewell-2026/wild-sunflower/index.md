*Helianthus annuus*

## About

Wild Sunflower is the ancestor of all cultivated sunflowers, native to North America and one of the few crops domesticated on this continent. In its wild form — rather than the giant-headed cultivated varieties — it is a multi-branched, prolific plant 3–10 feet tall producing many smaller, classic yellow sunflowers with dark centers. It is one of the most recognizable wildflowers in the world and is now naturalized globally, though its heart is in the North American prairies and open habitats. The wild form is far more valuable to wildlife than the cultivated single-headed varieties bred for large seed production.

## Wildlife Value

Wild Sunflower is exceptional for birds — goldfinches, chickadees, nuthatches, house finches, and many other species eat the oil-rich seeds voraciously. It is also one of the top pollen sources for bumblebees and specialist sunflower bees (*Melissodes* and *Dieunomia* species), which depend on sunflower pollen to provision their nests. It serves as a host plant for many moth species. Leaving the seedheads standing after bloom allows birds to feed through fall and into winter, providing food precisely when natural sources are becoming scarce.

## In the Treewell

The wild or species form of sunflower is the right choice here — not cultivated varieties, and the seed mixes should contain the true species. It will grow tall but generally self-supports without staking. Blooming from July until frost, it is one of the last things standing in the treewell as the season ends. Leave the seedheads standing as long as possible for birds; they will be stripped clean by goldfinches and chickadees within weeks of ripening.

## Growing Notes

- **Type:** Annual
- **Height:** 3–10 ft
- **Bloom time:** July–October
- **Sun needs:** Full sun
- **Water needs:** Low to moderate; adaptable and heat tolerant

## Seedling Identification

Wild Sunflower is one of the easiest seedlings to identify in any garden. The cotyledons are very large and broadly oval — among the largest seedling cotyledons you'll see — and they emerge quickly and prominently. The first true leaves are broad, roughly heart-shaped to oval, alternate, and covered in short rough hairs that make them feel scratchy. The combination of very large cotyledons and big, rough, broadly oval leaves on a robust seedling is unmistakable. If you see a big, vigorous, rough-textured seedling, it's probably this.
