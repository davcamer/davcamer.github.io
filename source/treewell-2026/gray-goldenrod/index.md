## About

Gray Goldenrod is one of the smaller, more delicate goldenrods native to central and eastern North America, named for the slightly grayish-hairy texture of its leaves and stems. It typically grows 1–3 feet tall with graceful, arching wands of small golden-yellow flowers in late summer and fall. Unlike many goldenrods that prefer moist, fertile soils, Gray Goldenrod thrives in dry, poor, lean soils — making it one of the most drought-tolerant goldenrods and an excellent fit for urban conditions. It spreads by rhizome but not aggressively.

## Wildlife Value

Goldenrods as a group are among the most ecologically productive plants in North America, and Gray Goldenrod is no exception. It provides critical late-season nectar for migrating monarch butterflies, native bees building winter provisions, and wasps. It is a host plant for well over 100 species of moths and butterflies. Sparrows and juncos eat the small seeds through fall. A common misconception: goldenrod does not cause hay fever — that's ragweed, which blooms at the same time but disperses its pollen on the wind. Goldenrod pollen is heavy and sticky, moved only by insects.

## In the Treewell

Gray Goldenrod is one of the best choices for a dry, urban treewell among late-season bloomers. Its preference for lean, dry soils and its tolerance of poor growing conditions make it likely to thrive where other plants might struggle. It will provide a burst of gold color in September and October when much of the planting has finished for the year.

## Growing Notes

- **Type:** Perennial
- **Height:** 1–3 ft
- **Bloom time:** August–October
- **Sun needs:** Full sun to light shade
- **Water needs:** Low; thrives in dry, poor soils

## Seedling Identification

Cotyledons are small and oval. The first true leaves are spatula-shaped (wider toward the tip), alternate, and covered in fine hairs that give the foliage a grayish-green color — the "gray" in the name is already apparent at the seedling stage. The basal leaves are noticeably wider and more spoon-shaped than the stem leaves will eventually be. The grayish, hairy, spatula-shaped leaves on an alternate stem are the key combination to look for.
