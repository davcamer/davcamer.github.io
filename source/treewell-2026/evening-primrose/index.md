*Oenothera biennis*

**Seed Mixes:** Eastern Pollinator, Northeast Native

## About

Evening Primrose is a classic eastern North American wildflower, native to roadsides, disturbed areas, and open meadows throughout the continent. It is a biennial — in its first year it produces a low rosette of leaves, and in its second year it sends up a tall flowering stalk, 2–6 feet high, covered in fragrant, lemon-yellow four-petaled flowers. The flowers open in the evening and remain open through the night and early morning before closing as the heat of the day builds. The scent is sweet and distinctive, carrying on warm summer evenings.

## Wildlife Value

The evening-opening flowers are primarily pollinated by sphinx moths (hawkmoths) that hover and feed at dusk and dawn. In the morning, before flowers close, bees make use of the remaining pollen and nectar. Evening Primrose also supports specialist native bees in the *Lasioglossum* and *Camissonia*-specialist bee groups. Goldfinches, sparrows, and other songbirds eat the seeds prolifically from the tall seed capsules through fall and winter.

## In the Treewell

As a biennial, Evening Primrose will spend its first year as a ground-hugging rosette and bloom in its second. Once it's blooming, it is tall and dramatic, adding real vertical interest. It self-seeds prolifically and will establish a perpetuating colony quickly. The evening fragrance is a genuinely pleasant feature for anyone walking past at dusk.

## Growing Notes

- **Type:** Biennial
- **Height:** 2–6 ft
- **Bloom time:** June–September
- **Sun needs:** Full sun to part shade
- **Water needs:** Low to moderate; tolerates poor, dry soils

## Seedling Identification

Cotyledons are oval and smooth. The first true leaves are lance-shaped with slightly wavy or finely toothed margins, forming a flat basal rosette that hugs the ground. The most distinctive feature is the prominent white or pale midrib running down the center of each leaf — this white stripe is clearly visible and is the key ID feature. Leaves are alternate. The rosette stays flat and low to the ground throughout the first year before sending up a tall flowering stalk in year two.
