*Rudbeckia triloba*

**Seed Mixes:** Eastern Pollinator

---

## About

Brown-Eyed Susan is a biennial or short-lived perennial native to eastern and central North America, found along roadsides, open woods, and meadow edges from New England to the Gulf Coast. It's often mistaken for Black-Eyed Susan at a glance, but it's a more prolific, branching plant that produces masses of smaller yellow flowers with dark brown-black centers. Plants typically reach 2–5 feet tall and bloom from midsummer into fall, covering themselves in hundreds of small daisies. The trilobed lower leaves give the plant its scientific name.

## Wildlife Value

Brown-Eyed Susan is an exceptional pollinator plant, attracting native bees, bumblebees, butterflies, and beetles throughout its long bloom. The Silvery Checkerspot butterfly uses *Rudbeckia* species as a host plant for its caterpillars. Birds eat the seeds in fall and winter. Because it blooms so abundantly and for so long, it provides a sustained nectar source that supports pollinators through the midsummer lull when other flowers have faded.

## In the Treewell

As a biennial, Brown-Eyed Susan will likely form a basal rosette in its first year and bloom in its second — so the first season may be modest, but subsequent years should be floriferous. It self-seeds prolifically and can quickly fill available space in the treewell. The masses of small yellow flowers are striking against the bolder, larger wildflowers in the mix.

## Growing Notes

- **Type:** Biennial or short-lived perennial
- **Height:** 2–5 ft
- **Bloom time:** July–October
- **Sun needs:** Full sun to part shade
- **Water needs:** Low to moderate; adaptable

## Seedling Identification

Cotyledons are small and oval. The first true leaves are oval to lance-shaped, opposite, and covered in short stiff hairs — similar to Black-Eyed Susan but the leaves tend to be narrower relative to their length, and as the plant develops the lower leaves often show shallow lobing at their base. The sandpapery rough texture is the shared Rudbeckia ID feature; smooth-leaved weeds at the same stage will feel very different.
