## About

Prairie Coneflower — also widely known as Mexican Hat — is a perennial native to the central and western prairies and roadsides of North America. It is unmistakable: the flowers have a tall, thimble-like or column-shaped central cone (often an inch or more high) surrounded by yellow ray petals that droop downward, giving the flower the profile of a sombrero — hence the common name Mexican Hat. Some forms have red-banded petals. Plants grow 1–3 feet tall with finely cut foliage and self-seed readily. It is extremely drought tolerant, having evolved in the harsh, dry conditions of the Great Plains.

## Wildlife Value

Prairie Coneflower attracts bees and butterflies to its flowers. Goldfinches and other small finches eat the seeds from the elongated central cones — the distinctive shape of the seedhead makes it easy to identify even after the petals have fallen. It serves as a host plant for some moth species.

## In the Treewell

Prairie Coneflower is well suited to the heat and drought conditions of an urban treewell. Its distinctive vertical cone makes it architecturally interesting from a distance, standing out among the more typical daisy-form wildflowers around it. It self-seeds readily and should naturalize in the treewell over time. Leaving the seedheads standing after bloom benefits goldfinches and adds winter interest.

## Growing Notes

- **Type:** Perennial
- **Height:** 1–3 ft
- **Bloom time:** June–September
- **Sun needs:** Full sun
- **Water needs:** Low; excellent drought tolerance

## Seedling Identification

Cotyledons are oval. The first true leaves are pinnately divided — cut deeply into several lobes or segments — with a rough, hairy texture. The deeply lobed leaves at an early stage and the rough hairiness distinguish Prairie Coneflower from simple-leaved seedlings. The leaves are alternate. It looks somewhat like Black-Eyed Susan seedlings but with more deeply incised leaf margins from the start. The overall effect is a rough-textured, divided-leaf seedling.
