*Centaurea cyanus*

**Seed Mixes:** Bird Lover's

## About

Cornflower is a European native that was historically one of the most common weeds of grain fields across the continent — so associated with agriculture that it's considered an archaeophyte, a plant introduced by early human activity rather than modern trade. It has been widely naturalized in North America and is beloved in gardens for its vivid, saturated blue flowers (also available in pink, white, and purple). Plants grow 1–3 feet tall with lance-shaped, silvery-green leaves. It's a cool-season annual that blooms best in spring and early summer before summer heat arrives.

## Wildlife Value

Cornflower is a rich nectar source for bees and butterflies, and its tubular florets are particularly well suited to bees with long tongues. Goldfinches and other finches are especially attracted to the seeds, making it an excellent "bird magnet" plant. Allowing the spent flowers to set seed rather than deadheading them maximizes the benefit for birds.

## In the Treewell

Cornflower is a fast-growing annual that will bloom within 60–70 days of sowing. It performs best in cool weather, so expect it to peak in spring and early summer, potentially slowing or going to seed in the hottest months. Direct-sow early — even fall sowing works in mild winters. It self-seeds reliably, so once established it tends to reappear each spring without replanting.

## Growing Notes

- **Type:** Annual
- **Height:** 1–3 ft
- **Bloom time:** May–September; best in cool weather
- **Sun needs:** Full sun
- **Water needs:** Low to moderate; tolerates dry soils

## Seedling Identification

Cotyledons are oval. The first true leaves are narrow and lance-shaped with a distinctive grayish-green color and a fine woolly or cobwebby texture — the surface looks slightly frosted or dusty. This grayish woolly appearance is the signature Centaurea feature and sets it apart from most weeds at the seedling stage. Leaves are alternate. The plant forms a low rosette before sending up flower stems.
