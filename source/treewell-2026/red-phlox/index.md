*Phlox drummondii*

**Seed Mixes:** Bird Lover's

## About

Annual Phlox — commonly called Drummond Phlox or Red Phlox — is a Texas native that has become a beloved garden annual throughout North America. It grows 6–20 inches tall and produces dense clusters of five-petaled flowers in vibrant shades of red, pink, white, and bicolor combinations. The flowers are highly fragrant, especially in the evening. Named for Scottish botanist Thomas Drummond, who collected specimens in Texas in the 1830s, it has been widely cultivated since its introduction to European gardens shortly after. It is a cool-season annual that performs best in spring and fall.

## Wildlife Value

The brightly colored, fragrant flowers attract hummingbirds, swallowtail butterflies, and hummingbird moths (sphinx moths) that can hover and probe the narrow flower tubes for nectar. Red phlox is a particularly good hummingbird plant in spring, when hummingbirds are first arriving from migration and food sources are still limited.

## In the Treewell

Red Phlox is best treated as a cool-season annual in New York — it will put on its best show in the spring and, if plants survive, again in fall. Sowing seed early indoors or directly as soon as the ground can be worked will yield the most spring bloom time. The vivid red flowers make a striking contrast with the spring-blooming columbine and golden alexander in the mix.

## Growing Notes

- **Type:** Annual
- **Height:** 6–20 in
- **Bloom time:** April–June; sometimes again in fall
- **Sun needs:** Full sun
- **Water needs:** Moderate; prefers cooler, moist conditions

## Seedling Identification

Cotyledons are small and oval. The first true leaves are oval to lance-shaped, opposite, and slightly hairy or sticky to the touch. The opposite leaf arrangement is consistent throughout the plant's life. Phlox seedlings are relatively modest-looking — the key is the opposite, slightly hairy leaves in combination with a somewhat sticky stem. Crush a leaf for a faint herbal scent. The plant grows quickly and the opposite leaf pairing becomes more obvious as it develops.
