## About

Foxglove Beardtongue is one of the most adaptable and widely grown native penstemons in the eastern United States, found naturally in meadows, open woods, and roadsides from Maine to the Gulf Coast. The name comes from the resemblance of the flowers to foxglove (*Digitalis*), though the plants are not related. It grows 2–5 feet tall, producing tall, showy panicles of white to pale pink tubular flowers in late spring and early summer. The foliage often develops attractive reddish tones in fall and winter.

## Wildlife Value

Foxglove Beardtongue is an important plant for bumblebees, mason bees, and other native bees that can navigate the tubular flowers. Ruby-throated hummingbirds also visit. Some native bees in the family Apidae are specialists on *Penstemon* flowers. The persistent seed capsules provide food for birds in winter, and the dried stalks offer overwintering habitat for stem-nesting insects.

## In the Treewell

Foxglove Beardtongue is notably tolerant of clay soils and periodic drought — conditions that often afflict urban treewells. It blooms in late spring and early summer before the worst summer heat arrives, adding height and structure early in the season. The tall flower stalks create a vertical accent that contrasts nicely with the shorter, more mounded plants around it.

## Growing Notes

- **Type:** Perennial
- **Height:** 2–5 ft
- **Bloom time:** May–July
- **Sun needs:** Full sun to part shade
- **Water needs:** Low to moderate; tolerates clay and drought

## Seedling Identification

Cotyledons are oval. The first true leaves are oval to lance-shaped, opposite, and notably smooth and hairless — even slightly shiny on the upper surface. They form a flat basal rosette. The smooth, somewhat glossy opposite leaves distinguish Foxglove Beardtongue from its relative Hairy Beardtongue, which has a noticeably hairy texture. The leaves are a rich, medium green without the gray-green cast of many other wildflower seedlings.
