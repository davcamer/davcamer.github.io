## About

Butterfly Milkweed is one of the most important native wildflowers in North America, and arguably the showiest of the milkweeds. Native to eastern and central North America in dry prairies, open woods, and roadsides, it produces brilliant flat-topped clusters of vivid orange flowers from June through August on plants reaching 1–2 feet tall. Unlike most milkweeds, it does not produce the milky latex sap in its stems and leaves. It is a long-lived perennial that develops a deep, fleshy taproot over the years.

## Wildlife Value

Butterfly Milkweed is essential habitat infrastructure — monarch butterflies can only lay their eggs on milkweed plants, as the milkweed is the sole food source for monarch caterpillars. Adults of dozens of species flock to the flowers: monarchs, swallowtails, fritillaries, native bees, and hummingbirds. The seedpods split open in fall to release seeds attached to silky fibers that drift on the wind. Few plants provide as much ecological return per square foot.

## In the Treewell

Butterfly Milkweed is slow to establish — it may not bloom at all in its first year, putting most of its energy into developing that deep taproot. Patience is rewarded: once established, it is extremely drought tolerant, long-lived, and virtually maintenance-free. Do not disturb or try to move the plant once it's in the ground. It should become more impressive each year as its root system matures.

## Growing Notes

- **Type:** Perennial
- **Height:** 1–2 ft
- **Bloom time:** June–August
- **Sun needs:** Full sun
- **Water needs:** Low; highly drought tolerant once established

## Seedling Identification

Cotyledons are oval, smooth, and fairly large for the eventual plant size. The first true leaves are lance-shaped, alternate (not opposite, which distinguishes it from common milkweed), and smooth — notably lacking the milky latex sap of other milkweed relatives. Seedlings emerge slowly and grow deliberately; at 2–3 leaf pairs the plant will still be small and easy to overlook. The narrow alternate lance-shaped leaves on a stiffly upright stem, with no hairiness and no milky sap when a leaf is gently broken, are the key combination to look for.
