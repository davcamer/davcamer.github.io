*Aquilegia canadensis*

**Seed Mixes:** Eastern Pollinator, Northeast Native

---

## About

Eastern Columbine is one of the most charming wildflowers of the eastern United States, native to rocky woods, ledges, and open meadows from Canada south to Texas. The distinctive nodding flowers hang upside-down from slender stems, each one a combination of red outer petals and yellow inner petals with five backward-pointing spurs. Plants grow 1–3 feet tall with attractive lobed, blue-green foliage that remains ornamental even after bloom. It is a perennial that self-seeds freely and gradually naturalizes into a colony.

## Wildlife Value

The backward-facing spurs of Eastern Columbine are precisely proportioned to match the bill of the ruby-throated hummingbird, making it one of the most important early-season hummingbird nectar plants in the East. Long-tongued bumblebees can also reach the nectar by hovering at the flower openings. The Columbine Duskywing skipper uses *Aquilegia* as its sole host plant for caterpillars.

## In the Treewell

Eastern Columbine is one of the earliest bloomers in the mix, bringing color in April and May before most other wildflowers have started. It tolerates partial shade under street trees, which is a real asset in an urban treewell where a nearby building or tree canopy may limit direct sun for part of the day. Expect the plants to go somewhat dormant and die back in midsummer heat, opening space for summer-blooming species.

## Growing Notes

- **Type:** Perennial
- **Height:** 1–3 ft
- **Bloom time:** April–June
- **Sun needs:** Full sun to part shade
- **Water needs:** Low to moderate; adaptable

## Seedling Identification

Eastern Columbine has a very distinctive seedling. The cotyledons are small and oval. The first true leaves are already compound — divided into three rounded, deeply lobed leaflets, each leaflet resembling a three-fingered mitten or a small fan. The leaves are smooth and a soft blue-green color. This three-part compound leaf structure is unmistakable and unlikely to be confused with any common weed at this stage.
